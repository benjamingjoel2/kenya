When people talk about the [Masai Mara](/destinations/masai-mara), they often mean two quite different things. There is the Masai Mara National Reserve, roughly 1,510 square kilometres of public protected land managed by Narok County, with the Mara Triangle in the west run by the Mara Conservancy. And there is the ring of private conservancies to the north and east, leased from Maasai landowners and together covering an area of a similar size.

There are no fences between them. Lions, elephants and wildebeest move freely across the boundaries, and the wildlife is broadly the same. What differs is how you experience it.

## Vehicle density

This is the single biggest difference, and for many travellers the deciding factor.

In the main reserve, any vehicle with a park permit can enter, including day visitors and minibuses from the many lodges along its edge. At a famous sighting, such as a leopard in a tree near the Talek River or a crossing point in August, you may find twenty or thirty vehicles gathered. Rangers do enforce rules, but crowding is a real issue in peak months.

The conservancies operate on a very different model. Each limits the number of beds, commonly to around one tent per 700 acres, and only guests staying within the conservancy may drive there. Most conservancies cap the number of vehicles at any one sighting, typically at five. It is entirely normal to spend an hour with a pride of lions without seeing another car.

## Off-road driving

In the main reserve, vehicles must stay on established tracks. Off-road driving is prohibited, and fines are significant. This protects the grassland, but it means you may watch a cheetah from a distance with no way to reposition for the light.

In the conservancies, guides can generally drive off-road to approach sightings, within strict guidelines that protect the animals and the habitat. The result is closer, better-lit encounters and far more flexibility.

## Night drives and walking

Night drives are not permitted in the national reserve. In the conservancies, they are one of the highlights. With a red-filtered spotlight, guides seek out the nocturnal cast: hyena on the move, servals hunting, bat-eared foxes, genets, the occasional aardvark and, if you are fortunate, a leopard on a kill.

The same is true of [walking safaris](/experiences/walking-safaris). Walking is restricted in the reserve but central to the conservancy experience. Guided by an armed ranger and a Maasai tracker, you learn to read spoor, identify medicinal plants and experience the bush at a human pace. It is often the part of a trip people remember most.

Conservancy camps can also offer bush breakfasts, sundowners wherever you like and private dinners out on the plains, with far fewer restrictions on where and when.

## Community land leases

The conservancy model is not just about exclusivity. It is one of East Africa's most important conservation innovations.

Since the mid-2000s, Maasai landowners have pooled their land into conservancies and leased it to tourism partners. In return, they receive a guaranteed monthly lease payment per acre, regardless of occupancy, along with jobs, education bursaries and healthcare support. Grazing is managed by agreement, so cattle and wildlife share the land in planned rotations.

The impact has been significant. Thousands of families now receive regular income from wildlife, and land that might otherwise have been fenced, farmed or sold has stayed open for animals to move through. Staying in a conservancy contributes directly to this.

> Every night you spend in a Mara conservancy supports Maasai landowners and keeps the wider ecosystem open. That is not a marketing line; it is how the model works.

## Cost

Conservancy stays are generally more expensive than comparable lodges in or near the reserve. Partly this is because of the low-density model: fewer guests must cover the costs of lease payments, rangers and infrastructure. Conservancy fees, usually US$100 to US$150 per person per night, are typically included in camp rates.

The national reserve has its own fees, currently around US$200 per adult per 24 hours in the July to December high season and US$100 in the low season. If you stay in the reserve or visit it from a conservancy, these apply.

In practice, the gap is narrower than it first appears, especially once you factor in what is included. Most conservancy camps include walks, night drives and some drinks, while many reserve lodges charge extra for activities.

## Where the reserve wins

The reserve is not to be dismissed. It has real advantages:

- **The Mara River.** Most of the famous [migration crossing](/experiences/great-migration) points are in the reserve and the Mara Triangle. If crossings are your priority, you need access.
- **Scale and variety.** The reserve encompasses a huge range of habitat, from the Oloololo escarpment to the riverine forest, and some of the most famous prides and leopards have territories here.
- **Choice.** There is a wider range of accommodation at different price points.
- **The Mara Triangle.** Managed by a not-for-profit since 2001, the Triangle is well policed and often less busy than the eastern side.

## Combining both

For most of our travellers, the best answer is not either/or but both. Two common approaches:

1. **Stay in a conservancy and visit the reserve.** Many conservancies sit on the reserve boundary, so you can spend a day at the river with a picnic, then return to a quiet camp. This is how our [Classic Masai Mara](/safaris/classic-masai-mara) and [Great Migration Special](/safaris/great-migration-special) work.
2. **Split your stay.** Spend two or three nights near the river in migration season, then three nights in a conservancy for walks, night drives and calm.

### Which conservancy?

Each has its own character. Some are known for big cats and open plains, others for hilly scenery or riverine forest, and some for closer cultural ties. The right one depends on your season and interests. We choose between them carefully, and our specialists can explain the differences in detail.

## Our view

If you are visiting outside the migration season, we almost always recommend a private conservancy. The experience is simply richer: more intimate sightings, more ways to explore, and the knowledge that your stay supports the people who share this land with its wildlife.

In migration season, a conservancy base with access to the river gives you the best of both worlds.
